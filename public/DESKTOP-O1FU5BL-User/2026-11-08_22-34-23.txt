  Different warm-up protocols effects on ROM: Ethical Concerns and Limitations 
  
  Ethical concern: Physical injury and discomfort 
  
  An ethical concern of this study is the potential for skeletal muscle or tendon strain when engaging in warm-up protocols and maximal glenohumeral joint (GHJ) range of motion (ROM) testing. The sample population of master athletes is especially susceptible to injury in this protocol due to age-related reductions in tissue elasticity and "stiffening of the muscles and ... their adaptability" (Fede et al., 2022). To mitigate this concern, Hirata et al. (2025) emphasize the importance of incorporating a pre-exercise screening tool to identify participants who may be a possible safety risk. Specifically, the PAR-Q+ (Warburton et al., 2011) will be completed by each participant prior to enrollment as it effectively stratifies risk assessment into two tiers: clearing low-risk individuals for enrollment and higher-risk individuals who require a clinical approval. 
  
  Limitation/methodological constraint: Sample size 
  
  A limitation of this study is the small sample size (N=30) as it reduces the reliability of the results and the ability to generalize findings to a broader population. Minimizing this limitation without recruiting an unfeasibly large sample size requires a crossover study design. This design effectively triples the number of observations, as participants will undergo each intervention thus making each individual participant their own control. However, a crossover design introduces the risk of carry-over effects, where residual physiological effects from one warm-up intervention may influence the subsequent tests as a confounding variable. To eliminate this possibility, the CONSORT 2010 Extension to Randomised Crossover Trials (Dwan et al., 2019) will be utilized by implementing a 48–72-hour wash-out period between sessions and counterbalanced, randomized intervention allocation across participants. 
  
  Consent: 
  
  Prior to enrollment, prospective participants will be informed of the study's purpose, procedures, physical and physiological demands, and potential risks; specifically, the risk of skeletal muscle or tendon strain that is associated with over-exertion during warm-up protocols and GHJ ROM testing. Following this, participants will also be informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without penalty. Written informed consent will be obtained from every participant prior to engaging in any screening or testing procedures. Additionally, participants will be required to complete the aforementioned PAR-Q+ assessment, validating their ability to participate safely. 
  
  Confidentiality: 
  
  To ensure the confidentiality of participants and their data is in accordance with research ethical standards (Canadian Institutes of Health Research et al., 2022), all identifying factors will be removed (e.g. names, institutional identification numbers, contact details etc.) upon enrollment and replaced by a unique numerical identification code. Files linking participants' identities to their assigned codes will be password-protected and stored separately on a secure server. Screening and data collection forms will be secured and only accessible to authorized primary researchers.

  One limitation of this study is the short observation period following each intervention. Since ROM is measured immediately after treatment and again approximately five minutes later, the findings can only demonstrate acute responses. Consequently, the study cannot determine whether the observed improvements would remain after several hours or develop into long-term adaptations. Konrad et al. (2024) reported that regular stretching interventions lasting at least two weeks can produce sustained improvements in ROM, highlighting the importance of distinguishing immediate changes from chronic adaptations.
  
  Another limitation involves the use of a universal goniometer to measure passive shoulder ROM. Although goniometry is commonly used in clinical and research settings, measurement accuracy can be influenced by anatomical landmark identification, joint stabilization, and examiner technique. Muir et al. (2010) identified measurement variability when assessing GHJ ROM, suggesting that small differences between interventions may not necessarily represent meaningful physiological changes.
  
  Additionally, recruitment through community centres using convenience sampling may create selection bias. Individuals who voluntarily participate may have different physical activity levels, mobility, or health characteristics compared with the general older adult population. Therefore, the results may not fully represent older adults with different levels of physical fitness or functional ability.

  ### Limitations
  
  One limitation of this study is that the participants' ROM is only measured over a short period of time. Since measurements are taken right after each intervention and again about five minutes later, the results can only show the immediate effects of each warm-up method. This means we cannot determine whether the improvements will last for several hours or provide long-term benefits. According to Konrad et al. (2024), stretching programs that last at least two weeks can lead to longer-lasting improvements in ROM. Therefore, a longer testing period would help us better understand how effective these warm-up methods are over time.
  
  Another limitation is the use of a universal goniometer to measure shoulder ROM. Although this tool is commonly used to measure joint movement, the accuracy of the results can depend on how the researcher positions the goniometer, identifies anatomical landmarks, and stabilizes the participant's shoulder. Muir et al. (2010) found that there can be some variation when measuring shoulder ROM. Because of this, small differences in ROM between the warm-up methods might be caused by measurement errors rather than actual improvements in shoulder mobility.
  
  Lastly, the way participants are recruited may also affect the results. Since the study uses convenience sampling by recruiting participants from community centres, the sample might not represent all older adults. For example, older adults who regularly visit community centres may be more physically active or have better mobility than those who do not. As a result, the findings may not apply equally to older adults with different health conditions, fitness levels, or mobility limitations.
